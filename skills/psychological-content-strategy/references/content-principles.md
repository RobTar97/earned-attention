# Viewer-first content principles

Use this reference when an opening feels generic, a script delays its value, or an edit adds activity without helping the viewer. It adapts the supplied `content-principles` skill into editorial decisions, not scientific laws. See [source notes](sources-evidence.md#supplemental-content-principles-source) for provenance and qualifications.

Apply these ideas while writing. Return the requested creative, critique, or production plan; do not append these headings, a psychology lecture, or a checklist to an ordinary script request. Explicit requests for analysis, scoring, or a beat table still determine the output.

## Start with one viewer and this particular idea

Imagine sending the video to one person in the stated situation. What do they already understand? What would help, surprise, reassure, or entertain them here? Use those answers to choose the detail and tone. Ask only about missing information that would materially change the creative.

Prefer a recognizable action or object over a generic audience announcement. “Still typing the same kickoff tasks?” can establish relevance without “Attention, agency owners.” Naming the audience is useful when eligibility, specialist context, or fit actually matters; it is not forbidden.

Understand the idea before choosing a structure. Try a topic-swap diagnostic: if replacing the product or subject leaves almost every sentence usable, look for a missing demonstration, detail, decision, or consequence. A reusable format can still be useful; repair generic substance rather than discarding a sound draft merely because its structure is recognizable. Consult [hook patterns](hook-patterns.md) for options after the premise and evidence are clear.

## Make the benefit worth the effort

Consider the value a viewer can recognize and the time or mental effort needed to get it. State a bounded, deliverable promise; show the useful action, result, or tension early. A truthful time estimate may help when the benefit is naturally time-bound. Check it against the actual demonstration or rehearsal.

More promised items are not automatically more valuable. One usable check can be better suited to a short than six rushed explanations. Do not inflate the count, invent savings, imply that a demo runtime equals the real task duration, or add an unsupported “in seconds” claim.

Curiosity is one reason to watch. Immediate utility, recognition, humor, craft, or emotion can also carry a piece without a manufactured information gap. Choose the reason that belongs to the idea.

## Give each line a useful handoff

Read consecutive lines together. Does the next line follow from a question, visible result, obstacle, consequence, or necessary context introduced by the previous one? If the draft makes a fresh generic introduction after a strong hook, replace that reset with progress on the promise.

Start delivering value rather than repeatedly announcing that value is coming. A demonstration can answer something immediately and then show how or why it works. Each beat should contribute information, evidence, orientation, emotion, or purposeful anticipation; every line need not add suspense.

A curiosity gap belongs to the viewer when they understand why the answer matters. An on-camera interviewer can help establish that question, but the act of asking it does not by itself establish viewer interest. Give enough context for the viewer to care, and pay off the question honestly. Humor can be the intended payoff when the setup supports that expectation.

Resolve supporting questions as the piece progresses and complete the main promise. Open a further question only when it follows naturally and has room for an answer. Do not withhold completion to force another view, or prescribe a new loop at a fixed percentage of runtime.

## Use concrete truth and audience-fit language

Replace vague summaries with available specifics: the actual screen action, wording of a task, relevant date, named source, or measured result with its conditions. Specificity must come from supplied or verified evidence. When a number is unavailable, show the mechanism; do not manufacture a precise figure to strengthen the hook.

Simplify the sentence while preserving the idea. Describe what happens in terms the intended viewer can picture. Keep useful technical terms for a specialist audience and explain unfamiliar ones when needed. A simple premise sentence is a clarity aid, not a universal word limit or proof that an idea is original.

Cut a favorite line if it diverts attention from the asset's promise, even if it is interesting alone. Retain background that makes the result understandable. Pauses for reading, demonstration, emotion, and breathing can serve the promise; silence is not automatically filler. Refine spoken rhythm through a read-aloud pass rather than a fixed average sentence length.

## Answer the live objection where it arises

Identify the most plausible reason this viewer would dismiss this specific promise. Perhaps the method seems to require unavailable skills, a large team, or an expensive tool. Address that concern beside the claim or action that creates it, using a relevant fact, demonstration, or clear limit.

Avoid an unrelated FAQ list or a defensive preamble. If a skill or resource really is required, disclose it or adapt the promise. If a video sells a product, make its usefulness and commercial intent clear; do not falsely claim it is not an ad. A supported boundary can answer doubt more credibly than a sweeping reassurance.

## Write beats the camera can explain

Attach a visible action, useful comparison, detail, or change in understanding to a beat when it helps. Those changes give the edit meaningful places to move. Use [video production](video-production.md) for timing, captions, framing, and sound.

Frequent cuts, chopped footage, and cutaways are options, not universal upgrades. A continuous shot can carry a clear demonstration or compelling performance. Keep a frame long enough to understand its evidence; change it when the new view advances meaning. Editing cannot substitute for a missing premise or payoff.

## Worked application

Fictional brief: LoopList saves and reuses checklists. A screen recording can show that workflow. There is no automation or measured time-saving evidence. The viewer repeats similar project kickoffs and may mistake a productivity-app promise for task automation.

Generic draft: “Busy agency founders, this game-changing app will transform your productivity. Keep watching for my favorite feature.”

Adapted spoken copy:

> “Typing the same kickoff tasks again? Save the checklist in LoopList and reuse it for the next project. It isn't task automation: the benefit is reusing the checklist instead of retyping it.”

The screen plan shows the supported save/reuse workflow early and holds the reused list long enough to read. The line about automation states a known limit at the point an inflated expectation could form. The ending completes its modest promise without a fresh cliffhanger. If the user asks for spoken copy only, return only the copy.
