# Hook library

Use this library when writing several distinct openings, breaking out of a repeated hook, or replacing a weak opening. Make the selection from the brief first, then use one or two relevant examples to sharpen the idea. For real assets and a reusable voice, follow [footage-first openings](footage-first.md) and the [project brand voice card](project-brand-voice.md). For production checks, see [short-form hooks](short-form-hooks.md).

These are original, fictional examples that illustrate eight approaches from the supplied playbook. Each set shares one brief so the differences in approach are visible. Use the user's requested count and output format. For copy only, return the opening lines. Add the frame, label, and next beat when they help the user choose or shoot.

Jump to [the starter index](#find-a-starting-point), [product examples](#product-demonstration), [software examples](#software-demonstration), [educational examples](#educational-or-creator-video), or [brand voice variants](#adapt-a-line-to-the-brand). ## Choose from the brief before writing

1. **Define the job and payoff.** Identify who should care, what they should learn, feel, or decide, and what the existing body can deliver. A discovery hook should make the subject and audience recognizable. A lead or sales hook should help the right viewer understand fit, proof, and the next step.
2. **Map the footage and proof.** Identify the usable first frame, the next beat, and what they actually establish. Distinguish inspected media, described media, feasible new capture, and missing material. Use an existing still, screen crop, caption, or available voiceover as appropriate. Treat a planned shot separately from ready-to-edit options. If the viewer cannot see or hear the evidence the hook depends on, replace the treatment or narrow the promise.
3. **Turn the brand voice into delivery choices.** Prefer approved copy, representative project samples, or the user's explicit voice cues. Translate them into diction, sentence length, point of view, energy, and how much emotion or humor to use. Respect words and constructions the brand avoids. With no sample, use a provisional register that fits the brief. Ask for a sample or asset detail only if the answer would materially change the direction.
4. **Filter, then recommend.** Keep options that can fulfill the promise with the available materials and intended voice. Within that set, choose the option that makes the goal clearest to the viewer and puts relevant proof or orientation earliest. For several options, vary the reason to watch or when the evidence appears. For one shared body, use the same promise and payoff. Explain the recommendation using a specific brand cue and asset when the requested format allows it.
5. **Write original words.** Choose a mechanism from the library, close the example, then write from the brief's object, action, and stakes. Read the line aloud or as a muted overlay. If the same line could serve an unrelated brand after swapping one noun, rewrite it around a recognizable detail, phrasing cue, or on-screen action. For text-only or copy-only requests, apply the selection silently.

A factual first-person story needs the actual speaker's experience. A customer story needs authorized facts and assets or a clearly identified reconstruction. With partial material, use a direct observation, a labeled example, or a present-tense demonstration that the project can deliver.

## Find a starting point

| Material in the brief | Consider | Adaptable line |
|---|---|---|
| A fair example where ordinary advice breaks down | Contrarian | “If [condition], try [supported alternative] first.” |
| A real mistake with a visible consequence | Prevent a loss | “Before you [action], check [specific failure point].” |
| An unexpected detail with a clear explanation | Curiosity gap | “Why does [specific detail change] when [condition]?” |
| A usable result, process, or artifact | Result or proof first | “Here’s [specific result]. Watch how [real action] gets us there.” |
| An informative motion, contrast, or sound | Visual or sound surprise | Lead with [relevant event], then name what the viewer just saw or heard. |
| A known audience question or task | Search intent | “Here’s how to [specific task] when [relevant constraint].” |
| A role or routine with a distinctive constraint | Audience identification | “If you [recognizable routine], here’s a way to [supported goal].” |
| A frustration the viewer will recognize | Problem recognition | “Does [specific frustration] happen when you [task]? Check [first helpful detail].” |

Choose by **what you can show**. A real result can support proof first, a recognizable symptom can support a diagnostic hook, and two matched shots can support a useful question or comparison. With sparse proof, use an observable detail, a practical question the body can answer, or a process the user can demonstrate. Treat proposed tests and missing shots as plans. Use [hook patterns](hook-patterns.md) for first-person experiments, confessions, and lessons grounded in the speaker's experience.

## Product demonstration

**Fictional brief.** A small maker sells a removable padded camera insert with a handle. The creator has a tote, the insert, a camera, and a spare battery. They can show the insert going into and out of this tote and retrieve the battery from a divider. The audience carries a camera on some outings and wants to keep using their everyday bag. Voice is warm and practical. The payoff is a visible packing method for this tote and gear.

| Approach | Spoken opening | First frame and optional label | First useful beat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contrarian | “Before you buy a dedicated camera bag, see what fits in the tote you already use.” | Show the familiar tote beside the insert. “Use the tote you have” | Seat the empty insert and show how much room this tote has around it |
| Prevent a loss | “Check where the battery goes before you zip the bag.” | Show the small battery beside the open tote. “Battery spot” | Put it in a visible divider and show the camera's separate spot |
| Curiosity gap | “How does the whole camera setup come out together?” | Show the packed tote with the insert handle visible. “One handle” | Lift the loaded insert clear of the tote and hold on the contents |
| Result or proof first | “Here’s a camera and spare battery packed into an everyday tote.” | Top-down view of both items in the insert. “Camera + spare battery” | Lift the insert out, show the divider, and put it back |
| Visual surprise | “That’s the camera setup coming out in one piece.” | Start on the real lift. Use the rustle of fabric or a quiet mix. “Removable insert” | Set the insert down and show that the camera and battery are still in their places |
| Search intent | “Here’s how I pack a camera in my everyday tote.” | Tote, empty insert, and camera on a table. “Pack a camera in a tote” | Place the insert in the tote and check its fit before loading the gear |
| Audience identification | “If you take your camera on some days and leave it home on others, try this setup.” | Hold the packed insert above the empty everyday tote. “For camera days” | Show the swap from an empty tote to a packed one |
| Problem recognition | “Ever reach for a spare battery and have to feel around the bottom of the bag?” | Start over the open tote with the dedicated battery slot visible. “Give the battery a spot” | Reach directly to the divider and retrieve the battery in the same shot |

Use first-person wording only when it fits the intended presenter. Keep the demonstration to the shown gear and fit. A packing shot establishes placement and access for that setup. Claims about protection, durability, or other bag sizes need their own evidence.

## Software demonstration

**Fictional brief.** A CSV import tool previews a column mapping before import. A sample sheet contains “Owner” as a header where the destination uses “Assignee.” A screen recording shows selecting the match and checking the preview. The audience is small operations teams. Voice is clear and low-key. The payoff is one visible mapping check before committing the import.

| Approach | Spoken opening | First frame and optional label | First useful beat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contrarian | “If the names differ, I’d check the mapping before renaming the sheet.” | Show “Owner” and “Assignee” side by side in the real interface. “Check the mapping” | Use the mapping control to match the existing header |
| Prevent a loss | “Before you import the CSV, check where the owner names will land.” | Open on “Owner” next to its destination selector. “Check before import” | Show the selected “Assignee” field and read the first sample value in the preview |
| Curiosity gap | “Where do the owner names go if the destination field is called Assignee?” | Show both labels, holding on the sample names. “Owner → ?” | Select the mapping and point out the same value in the preview |
| Result or proof first | “Here’s the owner name under Assignee, before the CSV is imported.” | Start on one matching input and preview value. “Preview the match” | Cut back to the selector and show the action that produced the preview |
| Visual surprise | “Same name. Different column heading.” | Match-cut between the same sample name under the two headings. “Same value” | Widen to show the selected mapping and where the preview appears |
| Search intent | “How to map a CSV column when the destination uses a different name.” | Show the real mapping menu. “Map a CSV column” | Select “Owner” as the source for “Assignee” |
| Audience identification | “If you’re importing another team’s spreadsheet, check their headers against your fields.” | Open on the sample file header and destination labels. “Match the headers” | Walk through the one mismatch and verify a sample value |
| Problem recognition | “The sheet says Owner. The app says Assignee. Here’s where to match them.” | Tight crop on the two mismatched labels. “Owner and Assignee” | Show the mapping selector and the resulting preview |

Use synthetic or authorized sample rows and keep identifying data out of the shot. A correct preview shows one mapping under the visible conditions. Keep claims about import success or savings scoped to actual evidence.

## Educational or creator video

**Fictional brief.** An illustrator teaches beginners how to separate foreground and background in a simple landscape. They have two grayscale drawings of the same composition. One uses similar tones for both shapes, and the second darkens the foreground. A recording shows applying that one change. Voice is patient and observational. The payoff is a grayscale check the viewer can try on a sketch.

| Approach | Spoken opening | First frame and optional label | First useful beat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contrarian | “Before adding details to that flat-looking sketch, check the two biggest shapes.” | Show the broad foreground and background of the first sketch. “Check the big shapes” | Point to their similar grays, then show the one shape that changes |
| Prevent a loss | “Before you ink the details, check whether the foreground blends into the back.” | Hold both shapes in the flatter drawing. “Check before inking” | Trace the shared edge and point out the similar shades |
| Curiosity gap | “Why is the hill easier to see in this version?” | Put the two matched crops side by side. “Same drawing” | Highlight the single darker foreground shape |
| Result or proof first | “One darker shape makes the foreground clearer in this sketch.” | Begin on the revised drawing. “Darker foreground” | Show the earlier version and mark what changed |
| Visual surprise | “Watch the front hill come forward as I shade it.” | Start mid-stroke in the actual shading clip. “Watch the front hill” | Hold on the finished patch beside the untouched background |
| Search intent | “Here’s how I separate foreground and background in a grayscale sketch.” | Start with the two drawings and simple “Before / After” labels | Compare the two shapes, then demonstrate darkening one |
| Audience identification | “If you’re drawing your first landscape, try separating the shapes before you add the details.” | Show the simple sketch at the outline stage only if available. The grayscale first version also works. “Start with two shapes” | Point to the foreground and background and compare their tones |
| Problem recognition | “Do your front and back hills look like one shape? Compare their grays.” | Show the area where the hills blend together. “Can you see the edge?” | Point out the similar tones and shade the front shape slightly darker |

Show the complete grayscale check and where it applies. Let the beginner see the difference before asking for a save, follow, or purchase.

## Adapt a line to the brand

For the same CSV preview, a calm operations brand could say “Before importing, check where Owner lands.” A warm teaching voice could say “Let’s check where those owner names will land.” A direct voice could say “Match Owner to Assignee before you import.” All three point to the same action and evidence. Treat these as tone variants of one angle when offering choices.

When the user asks for distinct hooks, vary the reason to watch, such as avoiding a mistake, seeing the result, or answering a question. When they ask to test openings against one body, keep that body's promise and payoff intact. Read the line aloud, check the frame against the available material, and name the next useful beat before recommending a hook.
