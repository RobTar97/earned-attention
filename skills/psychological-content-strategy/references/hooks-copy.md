# Hooks, catchcopy, and persuasive writing

Use this reference for openings, headlines, captions, sales copy, and copy connected to a video.

## Match audience awareness

| Viewer state | Useful opening | Body focus | Suitable next step |
|---|---|---|---|
| Has not identified the problem | Recognizable situation or surprising symptom | Make the problem understandable without exaggeration | Learn, save, reflect |
| Knows the problem | Diagnosis, relief, practical promise | Show a credible solution path | Try a step, compare options |
| Knows solution options | Comparison, objection, demonstration | Explain fit, tradeoffs, and proof | See details, try, request demo |
| Knows the product/offer | Specific benefit, new information, real offer | Remove uncertainty and make terms clear | Purchase, book, sign up |

This is a planning shorthand. Real audiences overlap; infer awareness from the brief rather than forcing them into a rigid funnel.

## Hook architecture

Align four channels: first-frame subject/action; short on-screen headline; spoken line; first sound. They need not repeat the same wording, but should support the same promise.

Formula: **recognizable viewer/situation + specific tension/desire + credible outcome + optional constraint**.

Weak: “Here are some app tips.”
Stronger: “Still rebuilding the same project checklist? Duplicate this template instead.”

Do not turn an unverified capability into a claim just to make the hook stronger. Use a demonstration that can actually be captured.

## Hook families

| Family | Adaptable template | Proof/payoff requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Result first | “Here is [result]. Here is how [process] works.” | Show the real result immediately |
| Pain recognition | “If [symptom], check [plausible cause].” | Explain limits and demonstrate the check |
| Diagnostic | “[Observable sign] may explain [problem].” | Provide a usable diagnosis or next step |
| Specific question | “Why does [unexpected outcome] happen?” | Answer the actual question |
| Demonstration | Show an action producing a useful change | Make process and outcome legible |
| Contrarian | “[Common advice] misses [important condition].” | Name the condition; avoid false certainty |
| Numbered promise | “[Number] ways to [specific outcome].” | Deliver that number within the runtime |
| Comparison | “[A] and [B] look alike. Here is the difference.” | Fair comparison with matching conditions |
| Experiment | “I compared [A] with [B] under [conditions].” | Actual methods and results, or clearly a proposed experiment |
| Confession | “I [real mistake]. Here is what changed.” | Genuine experience or labeled fictional example |
| Prevention | “Before [action], check [specific risk].” | Proportionate risk and practical check |
| Identity | “[Relevant audience]: here is [useful insight].” | Relevance without caricature |
| Open loop | “The obvious fix caused [unexpected problem].” | Resolve why; no empty withholding |

Generate different angles, not tiny synonym changes. Keep candidate promises compatible with the supplied evidence and final body.

## Hook quality rubric

Score 0–2 each: specificity; relevance; credibility; answerable curiosity; hook-to-body continuity. Explain weak dimensions when auditing. Below 7/10 is a useful internal rewriting prompt, not a validated predictor of views.

Some useful content relies on clear utility rather than suspense. It can score well through an immediate answer, demonstration, or precise question; do not add mystery merely to satisfy a formula.

## Title, cover, and thumbnail

Use one focal subject and one legible reason to care. Choose among:

- Direct subject/result: useful when the thing itself is distinctive.
- Before/after or unlikely comparison: useful when the difference is demonstrable.
- Relevant human expression: useful when personality, recognition, or emotion supports the story.
- Diagram or illustration: useful for an abstract process.
- Minimal text: useful to add a necessary question or label.

Use surprise, real quantities, or a difficult challenge only when the content justifies them. A title can extend the first frame's meaning but must not promise a different answer. For autoplay feeds, strengthen the first frame before spending time on a cover seen on other surfaces.

Check contrast, phone-size legibility, and UI overlap. Text placement and color meanings depend on composition, culture, brand, and surface; do not hard-code an upper quadrant or a supposedly universal emotional color effect.

## Copy structures

| Structure | Build | Appropriate use | Common repair |
|---|---|---|---|
| Benefit → proof → action | Specific customer outcome; substantiation; clear next step | Product captions, offers, short ads | Replace empty adjectives with evidence |
| Problem → consequence → solution | Recognizable issue; realistic cost; usable response | Diagnosis and problem-aware sales copy | Reduce melodrama; show the solution earlier |
| Before → after → bridge | Current state; believable improvement; how to get there | Demonstrations and transformation copy | Avoid implying unmeasured results |
| Attention → interest → desire → action | Relevant opening; useful explanation; concrete fit; CTA | Longer activation copy | Cut slow context and generic desire language |
| Customer → obstacle → guide → plan → action → outcome | Customer goal and useful assistance | Brand narrative, landing pages, case studies | Remove brand-centered boasting |

Treat structures as scaffolding. Finished copy should read naturally and need not announce framework names.

## Catchcopy and caption hierarchy

Use headline text to identify, promise, frame, or direct. Use progress labels to orient. Use captions to carry the speech accurately. Avoid stacking a slogan, subtitles, stickers, logo, username, and CTA as competing messages.

Example:

- Spoken: “The tasks are reusable. Duplicate the checklist instead of typing it again.”
- Headline: “STOP REBUILDING THE CHECKLIST”
- Caption: accurate transcription of the spoken line.
- Progress label, when needed: “1. Duplicate · 2. Assign · 3. Start”.

Keep important text brief and visible long enough to understand. Do not treat an ad text-speed recommendation as a universal caption-reading standard. Phone preview and language-specific readability matter more than a fixed word count.

## CTA design

Complete the promise, then choose the next action that supports the objective:

| Goal | CTA direction | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Utility | Save the checklist or try the step | Asking for a save without saveable value |
| Social relevance | Send to the person who faces this situation | Generic requests to share everywhere |
| Ongoing learning | Follow for the named recurring benefit | Withholding today's answer to force a follow |
| Discussion | Ask one substantive question | Engagement bait unrelated to the content |
| Consideration | See a comparison, demo, or details | Pretending every viewer is ready to buy |
| Activation | Try, book, buy, or sign up with clear terms | Hidden costs, fake scarcity, competing buttons |

Do not suggest “comment a keyword for the resource” unless a real resource and delivery process exist or are clearly planned. No CTA is necessary when it would weaken a complete story or the user requests none.

## Editing pass

1. Preserve the intended meaning and claim limits.
2. Move the customer outcome ahead of company background.
3. Replace jargon with familiar, precise words.
4. Cut greetings, repeated clauses, empty adverbs, and inflated claims.
5. Prefer active voice when clearer; retain natural phrasing when an alternative is awkward.
6. Use audience vocabulary drawn from provided material or verified research. Do not force slang from a dictionary.
7. Read aloud. Make speech conversational and headlines scan easily.
8. Confirm the CTA follows from the value delivered.

Readability scores and headline analyzers are optional aids, not proof of persuasive effectiveness. A Grade 6–8 target may be useful for some English audiences but should not be enforced on technical or multilingual content.

## Optional Japanese adaptation

Use Japanese only when requested or relevant to the supplied audience. Translate the intent and social tone, not English word order. Do not infer language from the workspace path.

| Intent | Example direction |
|---|---|
| Diagnostic | 「原因は、ボタンより説明の順番かもしれません」 |
| Practical check | 「公開前に、ここを確認」 |
| Specific outcome | 「伝わる見出しに、書き直す」 |
| Audience relevance | 「日本向けにアプリを出したい方へ」 |
| Honest curiosity | 「改善したのに、なぜ反応が下がった？」 |

These examples are directions, not universal Japanese persuasion rules. Match formality, audience, product truth, and voice. Proofread names, compounds, numbers, and line breaks manually.
