# Guided intake and brand-matched hook choices

Use this reference when the user is getting started, introduces a new brand without a usable brief, wants help choosing a direction, or asks to develop hooks together. Guide the creative conversation with specific questions and useful options. Keep a direct drafting, copy-only, audit, or analytics request in its requested mode.

## 1. Choose the interaction from the request

| Situation | Next useful response |
|---|---|
| “I'm new; help me get started” or an invocation without a task | Ask a short intake using the unresolved essentials below |
| Partial brief | Reuse what is known; ask only the missing facts that change the creative |
| Complete brief asking for a script | Choose a supported hook and deliver the requested script; include options only as appropriate to the request |
| “Show hooks first; I'll choose” or explicit step-by-step collaboration | Give hook directions and a recommendation, then wait for the creative choice |
| “Choose for me” or “just draft it” with enough subject/context | Choose and draft; label routine assumptions, without a hook-approval pause |
| Specific caption, translation, rewrite, audit, or analytics task | Return that deliverable; do not impose the video intake or hook menu |

Do not claim this is the user's first-ever use: there is no automatic onboarding history. Use the current conversation, supplied brand notes, and relevant user-provided project records. Do not infer this brand from an unrelated earlier business, account, device location, or source document. Store no onboarding flag or private brand profile inside the global skill.

## 2. Ask a small number of useful questions

Ask up to three short questions in a reply, in the user's language. Skip questions already answered. Offer concise choices with a free-text alternative, and let “undecided” be a useful answer. A host's question UI can help, but ordinary text questions must work too. Do not ask the user to choose technical framework names or complete a long marketing questionnaire.

For an empty first brief, a suitable opening is:

> What would you like to make: a video script, hook options, a rewrite, or a content plan?
>
> What is the topic or offer, who is it for, and what should the content achieve—education, relevant reach, qualified leads, or sales?
>
> What should the brand sound like: calm and expert, warm and practical, or direct and energetic? Your own wording or a short example is welcome.

These are starting questions, not a mandatory script. If the deliverable is already known, use that slot for a missing material fact instead. For example, a brief that already names its topic and audience may need only the goal, brand voice, or proof question.

Before promising specific outcomes, establish the relevant offer facts, real demonstration/customer evidence/experience, and claim limits. Before a timed script, establish or propose the platform/placement, approximate duration, language, and feasible footage/presenter. Ask only what matters now; combine related gaps compactly and do not repeat the entire intake. Unknown format choices can use labeled defaults. Unknown product results or personal experiences cannot become assumed facts.

Do not block useful work for optional colors, font choices, music, follower counts, competitor accounts, or a full brand guide. A brand URL, pasted wording, or described example may be enough; state when it has not been inspected. A reference to study does not authorize following, messaging, copying testimonials, or publishing.

## 3. Form a compact creative brief

Reflect the useful decision in one or two sentences: **For [viewer/situation], make [deliverable] about [true subject/offer] to support [objective], in [voice], using [available proof], with [format/resource limits].**

State only material assumptions and proof gaps. This is a working read-back, not a compulsory confirmation or permission gate. Ask again only if a contradiction or missing central fact would produce the wrong creative. Update the brief when the user changes it instead of restarting the conversation.

## 4. Build hook directions from the relevant material

Read [hooks and copy](hooks-copy.md) before proposing the openings. Use [content principles](content-principles.md) for one-viewer relevance, concrete language, and useful progression. Read the other references selectively:

- [Hook patterns](hook-patterns.md) when a genuine confession, experiment, counterexample, or lesson is available. Past-tense first-person claims require the speaker's actual experience; planned tests must sound planned.
- [Evidence to angles](evidence-to-angles.md) when customer material needs interpretation or the proposed promise exceeds the proof.
- [Psychology and brand](psychology-brand.md) for audience situations, brand cues, and brand-versus-sales objectives.
- [Short-form optimization](short-form-optimization.md) for a product/ad structure and its visible evidence; [educational scriptwriting](educational-scriptwriting.md) for a learning goal and useful depth.

Use the references to make decisions; the user-facing choices should describe actual creative, not internal filenames, jargon, or a lecture about psychology. Do not imply that research or footage inspection happened merely because these guides were read.

Default to three distinct, brand-matched hook directions, or follow the requested count. Change the opening question, proof path, or reason to care rather than only synonyms. Keep each direction compatible with the offered subject and a deliverable payoff. When testing hooks against the same body, preserve that body's core promise instead of inventing unrelated offers.

Each option should contain:

1. A short useful label and the actual opening words.
2. A first-frame/action idea feasible with the available material.
3. One concise reason it fits this viewer, brand voice, and goal.
4. The proof or payoff the body must deliver, including any material missing asset.

One compact table or three short cards is enough. Adapt the wording to the brand: a calm technical brand may use a precise diagnostic; a warm practical brand may use recognition and a useful action; a bold brand may use a supported contrast. These are options to judge in context, not rules predicting performance. Do not default to shock, shame, controversy, fabricated numbers, or false familiarity. When evidence cannot support a proposed direction, narrow it or mark it as conditional research—not a ready-to-film promise.

Recommend one direction with a concrete reason tied to relevance, available proof, voice, and production feasibility. “Best fit for this brief” is an editorial recommendation, not a promise of virality, watch time, or conversion. Do not append a numerical winning score unless the user requests a clearly labeled editorial rubric.

## 5. Respect the user's choice and keep moving

For an explicit hook-first or step-by-step collaboration, invite a simple response: a number, a combination, a revision, or “choose for me.” Wait before writing the full script when the user reserved that choice. A preselected UI option or elapsed time is not the user's answer.

For a direct full-script request or delegated choice, recommend/select the hook and draft in the same response when the brief is sufficient. Never add a generic “may I proceed?” approval step to authorized drafting. If the user supplies a complete brief after the initial intake and asks for the script, switch to drafting rather than requiring another selection round.

Carry the selected promise into the body and complete it before the appropriate CTA. Use [video production](video-production.md) for timing, voice, proof-asset status, captions and editing cues. Preserve the requested output form: a spoken-copy-only request stays spoken copy only. A plan or diagnosis does not need a hook-selection round unless useful or requested.

Offer one targeted revision after the artifact when appropriate: change the tone, opening, length, proof order, or CTA. Do not repeatedly re-ask known brand questions. If the user asks to retain brand preferences, keep a compact brief in the requested project location with source/assumption distinctions; do not silently create a global profile.

## Example hook menu

Fictional known brief: LoopList saves and reuses checklists; it does not automate tasks. Audience: agency team leads at repeated project kickoffs. Voice: calm and practical. Goal: qualified trials. Format: a 25-second Reel with a real screen recording of save/reuse. No measured savings or customer story is supplied. The user asked to see hooks before choosing.

| Direction and opening | First frame | Brand/viewer fit and promised body |
|---|---|---|
| **Show the operation:** “Same kickoff tasks? Reuse your saved checklist.” | Actual reuse action and resulting list | Recognizable recurring task and a concrete action; explain save/reuse and show the resulting checklist |
| **Clarify the boundary:** “Reusable tasks aren't automated tasks. Here's the difference.” | Reused checklist with work still to be done | Helps a buyer evaluate fit; demonstrate reuse without suggesting task completion |
| **Teach the decision:** “Before reusing a kickoff checklist, check it against this project.” | Readable reused list beside the new project's stated needs | Practical guidance; show the reuse operation and the human review it still needs, without inventing an editing feature |

Recommendation: the operation-first direction most directly uses the available recording. Invite the user's choice or combination; do not draft yet because this brief explicitly reserves the choice. If they reply “choose for me and write it,” select the supported direction and produce the complete requested script.
